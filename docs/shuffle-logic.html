One press of Shuffle answers three questions in order: which of the waiting players get a slot, who each of them is paired with, and which pairs face each other. Everything else in the function is a tie-break or an escape hatch for the two hard settings — forced teammates and nemesis pairs.

The shuffle only ever touches courts marked available. A court that has been confirmed is playing, and both the court and its four players are invisible to the shuffle until someone frees it. Players on break are skipped; players marked selected jump the queue.

getAvailablePlayers() · getBoundaryTieGroup()

Who gets on court

The queue is ordered by rounds played, fewest first — nothing else. Rank, win rate and waiting time play no part here; a player marked selected gets priority -1 and sorts ahead of the field, and exact ties are broken by a per-shuffle random value so the same two people don't alternate forever.

Take the top eight and you have the round. Two mechanisms are allowed to reach across that line, and both are careful to only trade players who are equally deserving — or to make the imbalance explicit.

Fairness invariant

The boundary pool only ever contains players on the same priority point. A rescue swap therefore never bumps someone who has played less in favour of someone who has played more — it only changes which of several equally-deserving players fills the slot. The force pull-in is the one exception, and it is deliberate: an explicit setting outranks the queue.

How strong a player counts as

Every strength comparison in the shuffle — quad ordering, partner rank gaps, court balance — runs through effectiveRank rather than the rank you set by hand. It nudges the manual rank by recent form, damped so that a lucky first game can't move anyone far.

resolveMode() · line 599

Choosing a mode — and falling through

Settings holds one of five values. Four name a strategy directly; auto — the default — rolls a weighted die on every shuffle, so a session naturally mixes even courts with lopsided ones. Auto rolls between three modes only. Novel is excluded: it ignores rank entirely, so letting it come up at random can drop a beginner onto a court of regulars. Settings offers no Novel weight to match — the three sliders total 100, and a weight saved before the change is folded into Mixed the next time the page loads. resolveMode renormalises regardless, so the roll stays correct even if the stored numbers don't add up.

The strategies are not peers, though. Tiered and Spread can fail: if nemesis settings leave a quad with no legal pairing, they return null and the switch falls through to the next one down. Mixed is the floor — it always returns a pairing — and Novel sits outside the chain entirely.

Tiered and Spread — the same machine, two seating plans

Both sort the eligible players by effectiveRank, cut them into groups of four, and pair inside each group. The only difference is how positions map to groups — and it is the difference between an even night and a lopsided one.

Inside a quad

Given four players sorted strongest to weakest, a split is just a choice of which two positions team up. Each mode brings its own list of allowed splits, tried in order, and that list is the second thing separating Tiered from Spread. A force pair inside the quad short-circuits the list entirely: the pair is locked and the other two become the opposing team.

Passes run strictest first: pass 1 demands that both partnerships be free of any repeat in the last five rounds, and only once no split can manage that does the ladder shorten its memory. Every pass insists on no nemesis partnership. There is deliberately no ceiling on the rank gap between partners — the quad is already the skill bracket, and the preferred split exists precisely to put its strongest player with its weakest.

SplitTeamsTieredSpread
[0,3] v [1,2] best+worst against the two middles — the most even teams 1st choice only choice
[0,2] v [1,3] best+3rd against 2nd+worst 2nd choice —
[0,1] v [2,3] the two strongest against the two weakest — lopsided by construction last resort —

Tiered's quads are contiguous, so its four players are already close in strength — even the lopsided split stays playable, and it is worth having as a last resort when nemesis settings block the other two. Spread's quads deliberately span the full range, so any split other than best-with-worst would hand one team the session's strongest player alongside a middle. Rather than produce that, Spread fails the quad and the switch falls through.

That single split has a second consequence. A mode with alternatives can always fall back on its least-bad option, so its ladder ends in a catch-all that ignores recency. If Spread did the same it would accept its one split unconditionally — recency could never influence it, and it would deal the same partnerships every round for as long as the pool held steady. So a single-split mode's ladder is allowed to end in failure: it stops at the last window that can actually reject, and a stale quad falls through to a mode that can still rotate. In a settled group this means Spread hands off to Mixed fairly often, which the court's mode label will show.

The pass ladder

Whichever splits are on the table, they are tried in order inside every pass — so the preferred split always wins, and a later split is only reached when the preferred one is blocked. The passes themselves relax the repeat-partner rule, strictest first, across a window that shrinks from five rounds to one.

PassesNo nemesisRepeat-partner ruleFires?
1–4requiredreject if either pair met within 5, 4, 3 then 2 roundsyes — the working range
5required… within 1 roundmulti-split modes only — catches the rest
6–10requiredreject only if both pairs met within 5 … 1 roundsreset histories only
11requireddropped entirelymulti-split modes, reset histories only
—requiredSpread stops after pass 4 and returns null: with one split, passes 5 and 11 could only ever rubber-stamp a repeat.

Pass 5's window of one round is vacuous, so passes 6–11 are unreachable in ordinary play — see the review notes. If no pass accepts any split, the quad tries a boundary swap (bringing in one of those equally-tied standby players) and, failing that, the whole mode returns null and the switch falls through.

Mixed and Novel

Mixed — strongest with weakest

Force pairs are locked first, then the rest are sorted and folded: strongest takes the weakest, then the next strongest takes the weakest of what is left. It is the simplest strategy and the only one that cannot fail, which is exactly why it is the end of the fallthrough chain.

The fold now carries its own recency ladder of 3 → 2 → 1 rounds. When the natural partner is a repeat within the window (or a nemesis), the walk steps inward for the next-weakest player who is neither, so the top↔bottom shape survives while the exact partner rotates from round to round. The whole fold is retried at each window; if none of them can satisfy every pick — the walk can strand a nemesis pair as the last two — the plain unconstrained fold runs so the round still happens, and the repair pass cleans up afterwards.

Because the fold is mechanical, it can seat a nemesis pair together. A repair pass afterwards cross-swaps one member with another pair, keeping the first swap where both resulting partnerships are clean; force pairs are never touched. If the configuration makes the constraint impossible — two forced players leaving a nemesis pair with nobody else — the pairing is kept and the conflict logged, rather than dropping players from the round.

Its memory is shorter than the quad modes' — three rounds against five — and it is given up entirely in the last-resort fold, but Mixed is no longer the mode that forgets.

Novel — fewest repeats

Novel makes ten attempts and keeps the lowest-scoring one. Each attempt locks force pairs, shuffles the remaining players with Fisher–Yates, then walks the list greedily: for each unpaired player, take the candidate with the best novelty score, hard-excluding nemesis partners and anyone paired too recently. If that hard filter empties the candidate list, the recency rule is dropped for that pick and only the nemesis exclusion survives.

noveltyScore(a, b) = max(0, poolSize − roundsSinceTheyPaired) × 100 teammate penalty + (opponentsOf(a).length − lastTimeTheyFacedEachOther) opponent penalty

The teammate penalty scales with the pool: in an eight-player pool, partners from two rounds ago cost 600; in a four-player pool the same pairing costs 200, because with four people repeats are unavoidable. The cooldown itself relaxes in phases across the ten attempts — attempts 1–3 demand the strictest gap, attempts 4–6 and 7–9 progressively less, and attempt 10 is the most permissive.

calculateMatchInCourtsRankBased() · line 1050

Seating the pairs

Pairing is done; now the pairs are matched into courts. The rule is greedy rather than globally optimal: take the first pair in the list, find the pair whose combined effective rank is closest to it, seat them together, remove both, repeat.

What each mode actually enforces

Force and nemesis pairs come from Settings; recency is derived from each player's teamateHistory. Coverage is no longer uniform by accident — but it is still not uniform.

Removed — the partner rank cap

Until recently every quad pass also required partners to be within three tiers of each other. It is gone: partner rank distance is unbounded, the [0,3] best-with-worst split is never skipped for being too wide, and Tiered and Spread now fall through only on a nemesis conflict. In a wide-open pool that makes Spread pair the session's strongest player with its weakest — which is the point of striding the quads in the first place.

Two different clocks

“Recently” means different things in different modes. Novel's cooldown scales with the pool — in a twelve-player session it wants roughly eleven rounds between repeat partnerships. The quad modes use a flat window that starts at five rounds and shrinks to one; Mixed starts at three. In a large session, Tiered, Spread and Mixed are all still more willing to repeat a partnership than Novel is, but the gap has narrowed.

Review notes

Findings from reading the implementation end to end, and from a per-commit review of the four commits that produced it. Five have been fixed; the rest stand as things to know.

fixed

A rescued quad was paired by stale positions

When a deadlocked quad is rescued, the standby player is substituted straight into the departing player's slot. Boundary candidates are tied on rounds played, not rank, so that slot rarely matches their strength — and the splits address positions, assuming best→worst. The quad was not re-sorted after the substitution, so [0,3] stopped meaning “strongest with weakest”.

Concretely, for a quad of ranks 1, 4, 6 and 9 rescued by a rank-2 standby: unsorted the split produced teams of strength 3 against 10; re-sorted it produces 7 against 6. One line, and the same sort regroupForcePairsIntoSameQuad already did for the same reason.

match-list.component.ts:809 · rescueQuadWithSwap

fixed

Spread dealt the same partnerships every round

Giving Spread a single split made its outcome fully determined by the quad: the ladder could only accept that split or fail, and its final pass accepted unconditionally — so recency could never influence it. A twelve-round simulation on a settled eight-player pool produced two distinct pairing sets, with two partnerships repeating every single round.

A single-split mode's ladder may now end in failure instead: it stops after the last window that can actually reject, and the stale quad falls through to a mode that can rotate. The trade is that Spread hands off to Mixed more often in a settled group, which the court's mode label makes visible.

match-list.component.ts:859 · formQuadPairs, and SPREAD_SPLITS

fixed

A player forced to partner themselves was seated twice

lockForcePairs looked up the forced partner by name and found the player themselves, pairing them with themselves and pushing somebody else out of the round. The Settings screen cannot create such a pair, but settings synced from Supabase are not validated on the way in.

fixed

The rescue pool could put the same player on court twice

getBoundaryTieGroup built its list of swap candidates from positions below the cutoff. Once the force pull-in was able to move players across that line, a player already in the round could still appear as a candidate — and rescueQuadWithSwap copies a candidate into a quad without checking membership, so a rescue would have seated them twice and dropped someone else. It now selects by membership instead, which also makes the evicted player available, since they are genuinely free.

match-list.component.ts:577 · covered by two specs in getBoundaryTieGroup — swap pool after a force pull-in

vacuous pass

The tail of the ladder is unreachable in ordinary play

The ladder runs strict-first over five windows, so passes 1–4 do the real work: a split is taken only when both partnerships are free of a repeat within 5, then 4, then 3, then 2 rounds. Widening the windows from three to five gave these passes considerably more to say — a partnership from four rounds ago is now rejected where it used to sail through.

What remains is the window of 1. recentWithin(…, 1) asks whether a pair met less than one round ago, and a pair's last meeting is always at least one round back — so pass 5 accepts anything the nemesis guard allows, and passes 6–11 only ever run when a player's teammate history outlives their round counter (history carried over while totalRoundsPlayed resets). An exhaustive check over every combination of pair histories confirms it: with normal counters only the either passes fire; the full eleven come alive only once elapsed rounds can reach zero.

Dropping the vacuous window-of-1 step would give the back half of the ladder something to do.

match-list.component.ts:818 · formQuadPairs

fixed

The fallback mode had no memory

Tiered and Spread fall through to Mixed exactly when constraints are tight, and Mixed used to be the only mode that never consulted teamateHistory — so a session that kept hitting the fallback kept reproducing the same partnerships, silently, because the fold is deterministic on effective rank.

The fold now carries a 3 → 2 → 1 recency ladder of its own and steps inward past a repeat partner. It still gives recency up in the last-resort fold, which is the right trade for a terminal mode: a repeat partnership beats a round that never happens.

edge case

Novel can return no pairs at all

If all ten attempts fail — possible when nemesis settings leave some player with no legal partner — bestPairs stays empty, and the shuffle writes nothing to the courts without reporting anything to the user. Novel has no fallback of its own, since the switch reaches it only by direct selection.

design

Waiting time is tracked but never used

roundsWaited is incremented every round a non-break player sits out, and reset on break, but calculatePlayerPriorityPoint only reads totalRoundsPlayed. Someone who arrives late has a low round count and jumps the queue ahead of people who have been waiting through several rounds. That may well be the intent — it equalises games played — but the waiting counter currently costs bookkeeping and buys nothing.

test gap

The pipeline itself has no test

Every strategy and helper is covered, but shufflePlayersIntoCourt is never called from a spec. The fallthrough chain, the mode stamped on each court, and the hand-off from the eligible window into the modes are all verified only by reading. That gap widened when Spread began failing on repeats, since the fallthrough is now a routine path rather than an edge case.

design

Combined rank is a weaker proxy for team strength than it was

Courts are matched by the sum of both players' effective rank, which read as a fair proxy while partners were capped three tiers apart. With that cap gone, a rank 1 paired with a rank 9 sums to the same 10 as two rank 5s, and the two are seated against each other as a perfectly even match — on court, the strong player simply covers everything. The win-rate tie-break only engages when the sums are within one, so it does not catch this either.

Nothing is broken; the metric just carries less information than it used to. Comparing the stronger halves of two pairs, or the spread within each pair, would distinguish these cases.

docs

The written docs describe an older algorithm

Both CLAUDE.md and docs/architecture-overview.html still describe three modes — balanced, mixed, novel — with weights of 60/30/10, a partner rank cap of three tiers, and a fallback from balanced straight to novel. None of that survives: there are four modes, novel is excluded from the auto roll, the rank cap is gone, and the fallthrough chain is different. Anyone reading either file will reason about an algorithm that no longer exists.
